# North star and guardrails

Business question: the new feature moved our main metric up, so should we launch it?

## The problem

Jobnest, a fictional job marketplace, tested a "quick apply" button that sends a saved profile to an employer in one tap. 1,800 job seekers were split at random between the current flow (control) and quick apply for two weeks. Lena, the product director, sees more people applying and wants to launch on Monday. The employer team is worried that one-tap applications are lower quality and that employers will stop replying. You will define the north star and two guardrails, compute all three from the raw data, and apply a launch rule that was written before anyone looked at the results.

The file `data/experiment.csv` is synthetic, generated by `make_data.py` with a fixed seed. Each row is one made-up job seeker: variant, applications sent, employer replies received and whether they unsubscribed from emails.

## What you will build from scratch

- `summarize(path)`: users, applying users, applications, replies and unsubscribes per variant, from raw rows.
- `metrics(summary)`: the north star and the two guardrail metrics.
- `decide(control, treatment, ...)`: the launch rule, as code, with its tolerances as parameters.

## The idea

A **north star metric** is the one number the team agrees best captures the value the product delivers, so that if it grows, the business grows for the right reasons. A good one:

1. Measures value delivered to the customer, not activity the company can inflate.
2. Is a rate or count of users, so it can move week by week and be compared across tests.
3. Leads revenue instead of being revenue, so teams can act on it.

For Jobnest the team picked:

    north_star = applying users / users     (share of job seekers who send at least one application)

No single number is safe on its own. Any metric you push hard can be gamed, by the product as much as by people. **Guardrail metrics** are the things that must not get worse while you push the north star. A marketplace has two sides, so pick one guardrail per side:

    reply_rate       = employer replies / applications   (are employers still getting applications worth answering?)
    unsubscribe_rate = unsubscribed users / users        (are we annoying job seekers?)

The **decision rule** is written before the test, with numbers:

    launch only if
        north star lift            >= +2.0 points
        and reply_rate drop        <=  2.0 points
        and unsubscribe_rate rise  <=  0.5 points
    otherwise do not launch

All changes are absolute (treatment minus control, in percentage points). A guardrail is a veto: a breach blocks the launch however much the north star went up. That is the point of writing the rule first. After the results are in, a big north star win always makes a guardrail breach look acceptable, and a rule chosen afterwards is a rule chosen to fit the answer.

A breach does not mean the idea is dead. It means this version does not ship, and the team either fixes the cause and tests again or makes the trade explicitly, with the people who own the guardrail in the room.

## Walk through

Step 1. Summarize the raw rows per variant.

```python
import csv
out = {}
with open("data/experiment.csv", newline="") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        s = out.setdefault(row["variant"], [0, 0, 0, 0, 0])
        apps = int(row["applications"])
        s[0] += 1
        s[1] += 1 if apps > 0 else 0
        s[2] += apps
        s[3] += int(row["replies"])
        s[4] += int(row["unsubscribed"])
print(out)
# {'control': [889, 269, 658, 133, 21], 'quick_apply': [911, 333, 1190, 156, 16]}
```

Step 2. The three metrics, from `python3 solution/solution.py`:

| metric | control | quick apply | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| north star (applying users) | 30.26% | 36.55% | +6.29 points |
| reply rate | 20.21% | 13.11% | -7.10 points |
| unsubscribe rate | 2.36% | 1.76% | -0.60 points |

Step 3. Is the north star lift real? Use the two-proportion z-test from phase 02.

```python
import math
ca, na, cb, nb = 269, 889, 333, 911
pool = (ca + cb) / (na + nb)
se = math.sqrt(pool * (1 - pool) * (1 / na + 1 / nb))
z = (cb / nb - ca / na) / se
p = math.erfc(abs(z) / math.sqrt(2))
print(round(z, 2), round(p, 4))   # 2.83 0.0047
```

The lift is unlikely to be noise. (The reply rate is per application, and one user sends several, so a plain z-test on it would overstate the precision. A 7-point drop against a 2-point tolerance is a breach either way.)

Step 4. Apply the rule. The north star passes (+6.29 against +2.0) and unsubscribes went down, but the reply rate fell 7.10 points against a tolerance of 2.0. Decision: do not launch.

Look at why. Quick apply users sent 1,190 applications against 658 in control, but employers replied to 156 of them against 133. Employers got 81% more applications and sent 17% more replies, so most of the extra applications were not worth answering. Over time, employers who stop replying make the marketplace worse for every job seeker.

What to tell Lena: "Quick apply raised the share of people applying from 30.3% to 36.6%, and that lift is real. It also dropped the employer reply rate from 20.2% to 13.1%, far past the 2-point limit we agreed before the test, so by our own rule it does not ship. The extra applications are mostly ones employers ignore. Next step: test a version that asks one question about fit before the tap, and check the reply rate again."
