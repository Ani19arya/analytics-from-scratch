# Metric trees

Business question: revenue fell this week, so which part of the business made it fall?

## The problem

Brightloom, a fictional home goods shop, made less money in the week of 12 October than in the week before. Priya, the head of growth, has a Monday standup and wants one sentence: did we lose traffic, did people stop buying, or did they buy cheaper things? Looking at revenue alone cannot answer that, because revenue is the product of several smaller numbers. You will break revenue into a tree, measure how much of the drop each branch explains, and then drill one level down into traffic channels to find where it started.

The file `data/daily_channels.csv` is synthetic, generated by `make_data.py` with a fixed seed. It has one row per day and channel (search, email, paid_social) from 5 to 18 October 2026, with visitors, orders and revenue.

## What you will build from scratch

- `build_tree(visitors, orders, revenue)`: the three branches of the revenue tree.
- `load_weeks(path, split_date, channel=None)`: prior week and current week trees from the raw daily rows, optionally for one channel.
- `decompose(prior, current)`: how many dollars of the change each branch explains, with effects that add up exactly to the total.
- `main_driver(effects)`: the branch that explains the most.

## The idea

A metric tree writes a top-line number as a product (or sum) of smaller numbers that different teams own. For an online shop:

    revenue    = visitors x conversion x aov
    conversion = orders / visitors
    aov        = revenue / orders        (average order value)

Multiply the right side out and the visitors and orders cancel, so the identity is exact for any period. Marketing owns visitors, product owns conversion, merchandising and pricing own AOV.

When revenue changes from R0 to R1, each factor changes too. The question is how to split the dollar change R1 - R0 between the factors. Because the tree is a product, take logs and it becomes a sum:

    ln(R1 / R0) = ln(V1 / V0) + ln(C1 / C0) + ln(A1 / A0)

Each factor's share of the log change is its share of the dollar change:

    effect_f = (R1 - R0) * ln(f1 / f0) / ln(R1 / R0)

The three effects always add up to R1 - R0, and the answer does not depend on the order you list the factors in. (The common alternative, swapping in one new factor at a time, gives different splits depending on which factor you swap first.) If revenue did not change at all, the formula divides by zero, so every effect is 0.

A tree tells you which branch moved, not why. Once you know the branch, cut it by a dimension (channel, device, country) and run the same decomposition on each slice.

## Walk through

A small worked example. Last week 1,000 visitors placed 40 orders worth $2,400. This week 1,100 visitors placed 33 orders worth $1,980.

```python
import math
prior = {"visitors": 1000, "conversion": 40 / 1000, "aov": 2400 / 40, "revenue": 2400.0}
current = {"visitors": 1100, "conversion": 33 / 1100, "aov": 1980 / 33, "revenue": 1980.0}
total = current["revenue"] - prior["revenue"]
log_total = math.log(current["revenue"] / prior["revenue"])
effects = {f: total * math.log(current[f] / prior[f]) / log_total for f in ("visitors", "conversion", "aov")}
print(round(total, 2), {f: round(v, 2) for f, v in effects.items()})
# -420.0 {'visitors': 208.09, 'conversion': -628.09, 'aov': 0.0}
print(round(sum(effects.values()), 2))   # -420.0
```

Traffic grew and added about $208, AOV stayed at $60, and the conversion drop from 4.0% to 3.0% cost about $630. The net is the $420 drop.

Now the lesson data. Sum the daily rows into two weeks, split on 12 October:

```python
import csv
sums = {"prior": [0, 0, 0.0], "current": [0, 0, 0.0]}
with open("data/daily_channels.csv", newline="") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        k = "prior" if row["date"] < "2026-10-12" else "current"
        sums[k][0] += int(row["visitors"])
        sums[k][1] += int(row["orders"])
        sums[k][2] += float(row["revenue"])
print({k: [v[0], v[1], round(v[2], 2)] for k, v in sums.items()})
# {'prior': [18051, 611, 34796.15], 'current': [18261, 530, 30971.09]}
```

Running `python3 solution/solution.py` gives the full tree:

| week | visitors | orders | revenue | conversion | AOV |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| prior | 18,051 | 611 | $34,796.15 | 3.38% | $56.95 |
| current | 18,261 | 530 | $30,971.09 | 2.90% | $58.44 |

Revenue fell by $3,825.06. Visitors explain +$379.92, conversion explains -$5,051.35 and AOV explains +$846.37. Conversion is the driver: without the conversion drop, revenue would have gone up.

Drill down by channel with the same functions. Search revenue rose $972.83 and email rose $1,263.29, while paid_social fell $6,061.18. Paid social conversion went from 3.11% to 1.27% while its traffic grew. The AOV rise is a side effect: fewer orders came from paid social, which has the cheapest baskets, so the average order across all channels went up even though no single channel changed its prices. That is a mix effect, and it is why you always drill down before you report a branch as good news.

What to tell Priya: "Revenue fell $3.8k week over week. Traffic was flat and order values were fine; the drop is conversion, and it is all in paid social, where conversion fell from 3.1% to 1.3% while traffic grew. Check the paid social landing page and ad targeting first."
