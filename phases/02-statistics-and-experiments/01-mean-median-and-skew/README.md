# Mean, median and skew

Business question: what is the typical posted pay for an analyst role, and which number should we put on the slide?

## The problem

Brightline Talent, a fictional recruiting firm, publishes a short pay report for its clients every quarter. Priya, the head of client services, needs the headline number for 300 analyst postings by Friday. Last quarter someone reported the average, and a client pointed out that most of their offers were well below it. Your job is to compute both the mean and the median by hand, explain why they differ, and recommend which one goes on the slide.

The dataset in `data/postings.csv` is synthetic. It is generated by `make_data.py` with a fixed seed, so everyone gets the same 300 rows. No row is a real job posting.

## What you will build from scratch

- `mean(values)`: the sum divided by the count, with no `statistics` module.
- `median(values)`: the middle of the sorted list, handling odd and even counts.
- `skew_label(values)`: says whether the data is pulled to the right, the left, or neither, by comparing mean and median.
- `read_pay(path)` and `summarise(path)`: read the pay column and report n, mean, median, the gap and the skew.

## The idea

The mean of n values x1, x2, ..., xn is

    mean = (x1 + x2 + ... + xn) / n

Every value pulls on the mean in proportion to its size. One very large value can move it a long way.

The median is the value in the middle once you sort the list:

    sorted list of n values, positions 0 to n - 1
    n odd:  median = value at position n // 2
    n even: median = (value at n // 2 - 1 + value at n // 2) / 2

The median only cares about order. If the top value doubles, the median does not move at all.

Pay data has a floor (nobody posts a negative salary) and a long tail on the high side (a few senior and director roles). That shape is called right skew. In right-skewed data the tail drags the mean above the median, so the mean describes a pay level that most postings do not reach. That is why pay ranges and salary surveys are usually summarised with the median.

A quick rule of thumb used in this lesson:

- mean clearly above median: right skew (long high tail)
- mean clearly below median: left skew (long low tail)
- about equal: roughly symmetric

"Clearly" here means a gap bigger than 1% of the median. It is a simple screen, not a formal skewness statistic.

## Walk through

Start with a tiny list and an outlier:

```python
pay = [60000, 70000, 80000, 90000, 100000]
print(sum(pay) / len(pay))           # mean 80000.0
pay[-1] = 400000
print(sum(pay) / len(pay))           # mean 140000.0, pulled up by one value
print(sorted(pay)[len(pay) // 2])    # median still 80000
```

Now the even case for the median:

```python
values = sorted([10, 1, 4, 3])       # [1, 3, 4, 10]
mid = len(values) // 2               # 2
print((values[mid - 1] + values[mid]) / 2)   # 3.5
```

Reading the column from the CSV:

```python
import csv
with open("data/postings.csv", newline="") as f:
    pay = [float(row["pay_mid"]) for row in csv.DictReader(f)]
print(len(pay), min(pay), max(pay))   # 300 43000.0 315000.0
```

On this dataset the solution reports a mean of 89,280 and a median of 83,000. The gap is 6,280, and 195 of the 300 postings sit below the mean. Eight rows are director roles paying 230,000 or more. Drop them and the mean falls to about 84,264 while the median stays at 83,000. The median barely notices the tail; the mean is mostly measuring it.

Recommendation for Priya: lead with the median, and show the range or the share of postings above a threshold if clients want to see the tail.
